The Revision Techniques Guide

Most students revise by rereading notes and highlighting them. It feels productive and it barely works. These are the methods that do.

Why rereading lets you down

When you read a page for the third time, it looks familiar, and familiarity feels like knowledge. But an exam does not ask whether a page looks familiar. It asks you to produce the answer with nothing in front of you. The only way to practise that is to do it: put the notes away and try to retrieve what you know.

Every technique in this guide is a version of that one idea.

Active recall

Active recall means testing yourself before you feel ready. It is uncomfortable, and that discomfort is the learning.

  • Blank-page summary. Write the topic title at the top of an empty sheet. Write everything you can remember for five minutes. Then open your notes and add what you missed in a different colour.
  • Flashcards. One question per card, answer on the back. Say the answer aloud before turning it over.
  • Teach it. Explain the topic to a friend, a parent or an empty chair. Wherever you hesitate is where you need to revisit.

Spaced repetition

Memory fades on a predictable curve. Reviewing just as you are about to forget resets the curve and makes it flatter each time. In practice, that means returning to a topic at growing intervals.

ReviewWhenHow long
FirstThe next day10 minutes
SecondThree days later10 minutes
ThirdOne week later5 minutes
FourthTwo to three weeks later5 minutes

With flashcards, use three piles: know it (review weekly), nearly (every three days) and missed (tomorrow). Cards move up a pile when you get them right and back to “missed” when you do not.

Mix your subjects

Spending a whole day on one subject feels thorough, but mixing two or three subjects in separate blocks produces better results. Switching forces your brain to reload each topic from memory, which is itself a form of recall. It also means that no subject goes a full week untouched.

Use past papers properly

A past paper is the most valuable resource you have, and most students waste them by doing them too early, untimed, with the notes open.

  1. Wait until you have covered the syllabus once.
  2. Sit the paper in one go, timed, at a clear desk.
  3. Mark it strictly with the official mark scheme.
  4. For every lost mark, write one line: was it a gap in knowledge, a misread question or a careless slip?
  5. Turn each knowledge gap into a flashcard and revisit it within three days.

Build the timetable

Count the days you have, subtract rest days, and divide your hours between subjects, giving a little more to the ones you find hardest. Keep the final fifth of your time for past papers. Write the plan as specific tasks, such as “ten questions on quadratics”, not vague ones like “do maths”. A specific task is easy to start, and starting is most of the battle.
